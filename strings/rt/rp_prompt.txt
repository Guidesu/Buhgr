<p><strong><em><span style="font-size:115%"> Welcome to Palimpseste! </span></em></strong></p>
<p> A quick primer on the setting. Nothing in here is law. Characters get things wrong, argue about them and believe whatever their grandmother told them, and that's how it should be.
<br><br>
Most of what follows is written the way someone in the world might put it. Where it matters for play, it says so plainly.</p>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> THE STORY SO FAR </span></strong></summary>

	<p>There isn't one story. It might start in a Skarnheim longhall, a Conjunct village that's lost three children to something in the well, a muddy town under Azure Peak, or a caravan camp in the Seamvale waiting out the rain. Wherever it starts, you won't be the only stranger there. Palimpseste is a crowded world and it moves around a lot.
	<br><br>
	What people talk about these days is the seams acting up. A shepherd near the Vyrlish border walked his flock home by the usual path and found a cliff. Fishermen out of Dunmoor pulled up a net full of fish nobody could name. Somebody's cousin swears the stars over the hills changed on a Tuesday. Most of it's drink talking. Not all of it.</p>
</details>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> THE PALIMPSEST </span></strong></summary>

	<p>Scholars in the Hinge have a word for the world, and it isn't a flattering one. A palimpsest is a page that's been scraped and written over so many times you can still see the old ink under the new.
	<br><br>
	Their argument goes like this. No god made Palimpseste. Lands came from somewhere else - other worlds, other planes, nobody's sure - and settled in one after another over ages, like silt. Each land arrived with its own history and its own creation story, and each one woke up certain it had always been here. The Achaeons will tell you their gods raised the islands from the sea. The Khemeti will tell you the first mound rose out of the waters of chaos. Both are right, as far as their own shores go.
	<br><br>
	Where two lands meet, you can see the stitching if you look. A coastline stops making sense. A road runs straight into a ruin older than the hill it's built on. A town on the border keeps two calendars because the two halves never agreed how long a year is.
	<br><br>
	Say any of this in a temple and you'll be asked to leave, or worse. Say it in the Hinge and people will ask why you're telling them something they learned as children.</p>
</details>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> GODS </span></strong></summary>

	<p>The gods are real. Pray right and they'll answer; blaspheme loudly enough and they'll answer that too.
	<br><br>
	They also didn't make anything. A god, the heretics say, is what happens when enough people believe in the same thing for long enough, or when one person believes so hard it takes shape. Gods can starve. There are shrines in the Vyrlands to gods whose names nobody remembers, and it's an open question whether anyone is still on the other end.
	<br><br>
	Every land brought its own. Tamarhel has its Nine Lights and the Daedric Princes who hate them. The Achaeons have their squabbling family on the mountain. The Ilé-Orun Coast has the orisha, who show up at festivals in borrowed bodies. Psyiadonia has the Ten, who inherited the world when the Allfather Psyiadon was killed, and the Inhumen, who want it for themselves. Each pantheon is quite sure it made the world. It's best not to point out that they can't all be right.
	<br><br>
	Since so many gods do the same jobs, priests talk about <b>domains</b>. A sailor doesn't much care whether the god that calms the sea is Poseidos or Abyssar, so long as the sea calms. A Death-priest of Haedes and a Death-priest of Nekhra will argue theology all night and then bury the dead exactly the same way.
	<br><br>
	You don't need a temple either. Plenty of families keep a god of their own, sometimes one that nobody outside the house has ever heard of. It still listens.
	<br><br>
	<i>In play:</i> you choose a domain and then a god who holds it, or you write your own. Your miracles come from the domain. Gods hear you best near a holy cross, ritual chalk or their statue, or somewhere their domain cares about - by the water for sea-gods, at the anvil for craft-gods, at a graveside for death.</p>
</details>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> MAGIC AND MIRACLES </span></strong></summary>

	<p>Magic is study and nerve. A mage picks a discipline - fire, frost, lightning, stone, force or something less polite - and spends years learning to push it around. It costs the body every time. Mages call it Strain: an ache at first, then blood, then burns, and at the edge the whole working breaks out of you at once. Mages who forget that tend to get short careers.
	<br><br>
	No spell is sure. Most failures just slip away; some misfire; the worst leave a mark on the mage for good. Ley stones and drawn circles make it easier. A desperate mage can burn their own body into a spell to force it through.
	<br><br>
	<i>In play:</i> every arcane spell rolls a spell check. Watch your Strain alert. Mages can also <b>Incant</b> - write a spell in their own words, the way priests pray. The Codex of Palimpseste in the encyclopedia has the rest.
	<br><br>
	Miracles are a different thing. A priest asks, and the god decides. Faithful service earns more devotion and deeper miracles, but nobody is owed an answer.
	<br><br>
	<i>In play:</i> anyone with devotion can <b>Pray</b> - write a prayer in your own words, name your god, say what you want and for whom. The god reads every word. Humility helps, orders usually don't (war and trickster gods excepted), holy ground and a devout life help more, and offering your blood or strength buys a stronger answer. Say the same prayer over and over and it stops meaning much.
	<br><br>
	Priests and mages mostly tolerate each other. In some places that tolerance is thin.</p>
</details>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> LANDS </span></strong></summary>

	<p><b>The Seamvale.</b> A long valley where half a dozen lands press together. Good for trade, bad for anyone who likes things to stay put.
	<br><br>
	<b>Tamarhel.</b> A whole continent. Skarnheim's jarls up north, the old Imperial City in Cyrodel, ash and dark elves in Morrowvale, courtly backstabbing in High Rook, high elves in Sumersette, desert swordsmen in Hammerfel, cat-folk in Elswyr, lizard-folk in the Black Marsh, wood elves in Valenwood. The empire over all of it has fallen apart more than once.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Vyrlands.</b> Birch forest going on forever. Leave bread and salt on the windowsill and don't whistle after dark.
	<br><br>
	<b>Varovia.</b> A valley under a mist that kills anyone who tries to leave, and a lord in a castle who is not alive. You got out somehow. Nobody believes you.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Conjunct.</b> Northern kingdoms that have been at war with each other and with monsters for as long as anyone remembers. They hire mutated slayers to deal with the monsters and don't thank them.
	<br><br>
	<b>Tír Aenna.</b> Rain, stone walls and hills that aren't always hills. Be careful what you promise. Be more careful what you accept.
	<br><br>
	<b>Faerhen.</b> Wizard towers, adventuring companies with charters, and a god for everything. If there's a dungeon, someone in Faerhen has a map of it for sale.
	<br><br>
	<b>Thessadra.</b> Mages live in towers under guard, and there are good reasons for it. Demons wait on the other side of dreams.
	<br><br>
	<b>Golaren.</b> More nations than you can count, and scholarly lodges that insist on counting anyway.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Laurentine Dominion.</b> Roads, legions, aqueducts and lawyers. Any god is welcome so long as it's registered and pays tax.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Achaeon Isles.</b> City-states, heroes and a family of gods who can't leave mortals alone. The door to the underworld is somewhere in the hills.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Khemet Sands.</b> One great river and a lot of desert. The kings build tombs the size of cities and the grave-robbers keep very good notes.
	<br><br>
	<b>Athrae.</b> Green once, until its sorcerer-kings burned the life out of the land for power. Using magic openly there will get you killed.
	<br><br>
	<b>Ashurim.</b> Brick cities between two rivers. They wrote the first laws down and have been suing each other ever since. Most great houses keep a djinn on contract.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Ilé-Orun Coast.</b> Trading kingdoms, bronze-casters and griots who can recite your family back forty generations. The spider owns all the stories.
	<br><br>
	<b>Turtle's Back.</b> Prairie and river country resting on the first turtle's shell. Spirit-walkers talk to the animal nations. Some hungers in the winter woods are not talked about.
	<br><br>
	<b>Hinomura.</b> Islands full of kami, shrines and very polite people with very sharp swords. When the lanterns go out, the yokai come.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Dunmoor Isles.</b> Grey cities that burn whale oil and cough up plague. Rich people live behind walls of light. Something whispers from carved bone.
	<br><br>
	<b>Yharrow.</b> A gothic city whose church heals any sickness with blood. Don't ask where the blood comes from. Stay indoors on the night of the hunt.
	<br><br>
	<b>Psyiadonia.</b> An old, hard world whose creator was murdered. Its people tithe to the Ten, burn heretics and live one bad winter from ruin. The Grand Duchy of Azuria and Azure Peak are here.
	<br><br>
	<b>The Hinge.</b> A city with no sky, where any doorway might open onto anywhere. Everyone there knows how the world was made and finds it a bit boring.
	<br><br>
	<b>Elsewhere.</b> A village too small to name, a wagon, a patch of land nobody claims. Some people come from nowhere in particular and like it that way.</p>
</details>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> TONGUES </span></strong></summary>

	<p>Most lands have their own language: Tamarhelic, Vyrlish, Nordling, Aennach, Faerhenic, Thessic, Laurentine, Achaean, Khemeti, Ashurite, Oruni, Hinomuran, Shelltongue, Dunmoorish. Clergy nearly everywhere learn Laurentine for the old texts, whether they like it or not.
	<br><br>
	Travellers mostly get by in <b>Seamspeak</b>, a trade pidgin cobbled together at the borders. No one grows up speaking it. Everyone ends up speaking some.</p>
</details>

<details>
	<summary><strong><span style="font-size:130%"> WORDS YOU'LL HEAR </span></strong></summary>

	<p><b>Palimpseste</b> - the world.
	<br><b>Seam</b> - a place where two lands meet and don't quite fit.
	<br><b>The Hinge</b> - the city of doors.
	<br><b>Domain</b> - what a god has power over. Sea, Death, Craft and so on.
	<br><b>Private devotion</b> - worshipping a god no church knows. It still counts.
	<br><b>The Ten</b> - Psyiadonia's gods: Astratha, Nokk, Dendhor, Abyssar, Ravokh, Nekhra, Zylix, Pestrah, Malumm, Eorah.
	<br><b>The Inhumen</b> - the ones Psyiadonia burns people for: Zyzo, Gragghar, Mathios, Baothe.
	<br><b>Elsewhere</b> - where you're from if you'd rather not say.</p>
</details>
